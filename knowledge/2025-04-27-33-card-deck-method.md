# This 33 Card Deck Will Change How You Build Decks Forever

Source: Based Deck Department, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s4_rpbUbz6E (transcript only). Worked example: Sauron spellslinger. The author's spreadsheet template was not ingested.

## The idea

In an average game you draw about a third of the deck. So build one 33-card deck that does the thing, tune it, then replicate it two more times with other cards. Small decks, as in limited formats, have no room to hide: a curve that is too high, too little card advantage, or too few lands is felt immediately.

## Method

1. Write what the deck does in the early game and how it wins (example: land drops plus two ramp for the commander on turn 4, turn 5 at the latest; then three to four turns behind a growing board; then the kill).
2. Turn that into the cards wanted **across one game**, as counts per role. The example's single-game shopping list: 3 card draw, 3 single-target removal, 1 spell copy, 1 army growth, 1 fling effect, 1 ring-tempt source, 1 evasion source, 1 cost reducer, 1 board wipe, 1 counterspell, 1 graveyard recursion, plus enough ramp and lands to cast it all.
3. For an existing deck: split the 99 into three 33s, each with the same role counts.
4. For a new deck: build one 33 first, test it, then copy its role counts twice.

## What the split reveals

- **Uneven roles.** A role whose count does not divide by three means one third is short a land, a ramp piece, or a counterspell. Fix the count.
- **One-offs.** A card with no counterpart in the other two thirds is outside the core focus, even if it is good (example: Archmage Emeritus). Cut it or commit to three.
- **True theme density.** Seeing a theme laid out per third shows how many copies there really are and so how likely they are to be drawn.
- **Value engines standing in for a specific need.** Replace a generic value card with the exact effect the plan requires (example: Exalted Flamer of Tzeentch replaced by specific recursion).

## Why test the 33

- Less variance, so a goldfish or a real game shows the core plan in its ideal form quickly and reliably.
- It makes the problem small enough for new players.
- People want uniqueness, not randomness. A deck's trick only counts if it happens reliably.
